# CHAPTER 2
## REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES

This chapter surveys literature from 2015 onwards in three groups: (a) structural-response theory as recently applied to simplified multistory models, ground-motion characterization, and drift reporting; (b) technical approaches to interactive seismic visualization, including browser-based, virtual-reality, digital-twin, and damage-visualization methods; and (c) educational and usability evidence for interactive earthquake-learning media and for the System Usability Scale as an evaluation instrument. It closes with a synthesis and the research gap addressed by QuakeStudio. No source cited in this chapter predates 2015.

### Theoretical Background

**Idealized shear-building models for multistory response.** Zuo et al. (2022) examined the rational use of idealized shear-building models to approximate actual buildings and characterized them as a low-computational-cost simplification for multistory seismic response. Their treatment supports the present choice of a lumped-mass shear idealization for an educational prototype: floor masses connected by elements representing lateral story stiffness, with damping included, solved as M·ü + C·u̇ + K·u = −M·1·a_g(t). Zuo et al. (2022) also caution that shear-building idealizations can differ noticeably from detailed models, which is why the present study restricts interpretation to within the simplification, subjects every coefficient to adviser review, and validates code fidelity against an independent solution of the same model rather than against a real structure.

**Stiffness calibration from an empirical period.** The prototype follows a stated two-step procedure: estimate the fundamental period from T1 = Ct·H^x, then calibrate uniform story stiffness in closed form, k = m·ω1² / (4·sin²(π / (2(2N + 1)))), where ω1 = 2π/T1, m is floor mass, and N is the number of floors. The derivation for a uniform shear building is recorded in the project assumptions record for reviewer checking. A documented limitation is that the implemented period coefficients (Ct = 0.072, x = 0.8) are identical for concrete, steel, composite, and wood, which is almost certainly incorrect and is identified as the weakest point in the model for adviser correction. A further documented point is that the frame stiffness multiplier (braced = 2.0× moment) is applied after period targeting, so the empirical formula effectively describes the moment-frame case only.

**Damping.** Rayleigh damping, C = a0·M + a1·K with a0 = 2ζω1ω2/(ω1 + ω2) and a1 = 2ζ/(ω1 + ω2), is fitted to the material damping ratio at the first two modes, with a mass-proportional fallback for a single floor. Implemented ratios are 2–5% by material as placeholders. The treatment is consistent with standard multistory idealizations reviewed in recent shear-model literature (Zuo et al., 2022).

**Ground motion and intensity.** The U.S. Geological Survey (2022) defines ground motion as shaking of the ground during earthquakes and distinguishes magnitude, energy release, and shaking intensity as related but distinct characterizations. The prototype operationalizes shaking as synthetic acceleration histories scaled to an exact peak ground acceleration (0.05–0.60 g), with harmonic, band-limited random (seeded), and impulse presets, duration 5–60 s, and taper to rest at both ends. Recorded accelerograms are excluded from the prototype to keep it self-contained and licensing-free; the random preset is deterministic from its seed so that runs are reproducible and cross-validation is meaningful.

**Drift as a reported response quantity.** Ji et al. (2023) addressed enhanced measurement of structural interstory drift responses in shaking-table tests, confirming drift as a standard response quantity in experimental seismic assessment. The prototype reports per-story drift ratio and maximum drift ratio with worst-story index. The implementation uses difference of per-floor peaks divided by story height, which is documented as differing from simultaneous (time-coincident) drift and is flagged for adviser review. A measurement study through the shipping engine (84 runs across templates and PGA 0.05–0.60 g) found that the shipped drift colour bands placed 74% of runs in one band and labeled the default 5-story building "very large" above 0.10 g, motivating a recommended logarithmic scale. This finding is reported rather than concealed, and the bands are presented as display thresholds, not as safety judgments.

**Lateral displacement as a comparative quantity.** Souhaibou and Li (2023) compared lateral displacement of a multistory reinforced-concrete building under wind and earthquake actions using the base-shear method and ETABS software, illustrating the practice of comparing computed lateral displacement across analysis approaches for the same building. The present study inverts that logic for pedagogy: it holds the ground motion fixed and varies the building, comparing peak displacement and drift across configurations under the same input.

### Related Studies

**Multidimensional analysis with visualization.** Hsu et al. (2022) developed and applied a platform for multidimensional urban earthquake impact simulation, connecting dynamic building analysis with visualization to present calculated seismic responses. Their work establishes the instructional value of displaying computed response rather than reporting scalars alone, which the present study adopts at building scale: every computed floor history drives both the 3D sway and the charts from the same result array.

**Browser-based interactive seismic visualization.** Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated a WebGL-based interactive visualization framework for large-scale urban seismic simulations with a dual multi-level-of-detail strategy, showing that the browser without specialized software installation is a viable host for interactive seismic visualization. QuakeStudio applies that delivery precedent to a single-building educational scope: Three.js rendering, lazy-loaded 3D and chart chunks sharing one 3D chunk, animation writes to scene transforms without React renders, and a single clock samples the same displacement array for sway and chart cursor.

**Finite-element analysis with virtual reality.** Isobe and Yang (2024) integrated finite element analysis with virtual reality for structural and indoor nonstructural components under seismic excitation. Their integration shows the upper bound of fidelity available when detailed analysis and immersive display are combined. The present study deliberately does not pursue that bound: detailed finite-element requirements and integration cost exceed a two-to-three-month student prototype, so a shear idealization with a standard 3D view is substituted and its limits are stated.

**Game-engine digital twins.** Jiang et al. (2025) combined building information modeling, monitoring data, and physical models in a game-engine-empowered digital twin for seismic damage simulation of buildings. Their framework shows the value of linking information models with live or monitored behavior. QuakeStudio retains only the comparative intent — same input, different configurations — without building information modeling, sensor data, or damage simulation, which remain outside scope.

**Seismic damage visualization tools.** Naraghi et al. (2024) systematically reviewed tools and interoperability considerations in simulating and visualizing indoor seismic damage. Their review confirms that tool choice and data exchange constrain what can be shown and shared. The present study responds by minimizing those constraints: synthetic inputs, deterministic outputs, CSV export in displayed units at full precision from the same result the charts render, and JSON run export/import with per-run validation, so that a file and the screen cannot disagree.

**Virtual-reality media for earthquake-resistant construction learning.** Kuncoro et al. (2023) reported favorable learning outcomes from virtual-reality-based learning media on earthquake-resistant construction for civil engineering students. Their findings motivate investigating the educational potential of interactive structural-motion presentation and guided student exploration. The present study does not replicate their learning-gains claim; any claim of improved learning is deferred to a separate, appropriate learning assessment, and the present evaluation is limited to calculation agreement and to users' ability to configure, run, and interpret simulations.

**Usability evaluation with the System Usability Scale.** Escaldelai et al. (2024) applied the System Usability Scale in a methodological usability study of a support system, illustrating its continued use as a widely adopted usability instrument scored on a 0–100 scale with task success and completion time as complementary measures. The present study adopts the same trio — task success rate, task completion time, and mean and standard deviation of questionnaire scores — for its deferred user trials (build a model, run a simulation, compare two configurations). A documented scoring trap is handled explicitly: scoring is by item parity (odd items value − 1, even items 5 − value, summed and multiplied by 2.5), keyed off item number, because two items' wording polarity does not match their scoring direction and a wording-based correction would cost 10 points per response. The rule is implemented twice (TypeScript for participant display, Python for researcher reporting) and cross-checked on 10 cases for exact agreement.

### Synthesis and Research Gap

Recent work converges on three points. First, simplified shear idealizations remain a legitimate low-cost basis for approximating multistory response when their limits are stated and their coefficients are reviewed (Zuo et al., 2022; Ji et al., 2023; Souhaibou & Li, 2023). Second, coupling calculation with visualization — whether multidimensional platforms (Hsu et al., 2022), browser frameworks (Wang et al., 2025), virtual reality (Isobe & Yang, 2024), or digital twins (Jiang et al., 2025) — makes response inspectable, subject to tool and interoperability choices (Naraghi et al., 2024). Third, interactive media show educational promise (Kuncoro et al., 2023) best substantiated through proper usability and learning evaluation (Escaldelai et al., 2024).

The gap is a verified, small-scope bridge between those strands: a browser tool whose simplified structural calculations are presented through an interactive educational website with stated assumptions, deterministic outputs, animation-graph consistency, same-motion comparison, and gated verification — without claiming damage prediction, safety certification, or measured learning gains. Existing detailed approaches (Isobe & Yang, 2024; Jiang et al., 2025) provide precedents but their analysis and integration requirements extend beyond the intended student prototype. QuakeStudio is positioned in that gap: it simplifies interaction and modeling requirements while retaining a clear, reviewable relationship between calculations and displayed motion.
